If you owned something before you got married, or you received a gift or inheritance during the marriage, you'd probably assume it's simply yours to keep in a divorce. Usually, that's true. But there are specific, well-defined ways that "yours" can quietly turn into "ours" without either spouse meaning for it to happen — and this article walks through exactly what those are, in plain language.

**Quick Answer: What Stays Separate Property**
- Property you owned **before marriage**, and gifts or **inheritance** you receive **during marriage** (even from your spouse's family), generally stay your separate property
- It can lose that protection through **commingling** (mixing separate and shared money together until they can't be told apart) or **transmutation** (a formal, written agreement to change what something legally is)
- **A verbal agreement to change ownership doesn't count** — California law requires a signed, written document
- If community (shared marital) money helps pay down a separate property house, the marriage may earn back a partial financial interest through a specific formula called **Moore/Marsden**
- **Keeping clean records** — bank statements, gift letters, inheritance paperwork — is the single best protection for separate property

## What "Separate Property" Actually Means

Under California Family Code § 770, separate property generally includes:

- Anything you owned **before** the marriage
- Anything you received as a **gift** or **inheritance**, even if it happened during the marriage
- Money you earn **after** the official date of separation

This is the flip side of [community property](/posts/community-property-division-california/), which covers everything acquired *during* the marriage through work or shared effort. Separate property is meant to stay with the person it belongs to — but California law has specific rules about how it can accidentally change categories, and that's what the rest of this article covers. The same date-of-separation concept also matters for [retirement accounts specifically](/posts/qdro-retirement-division-california/), where contributions made after that date generally stay separate too.

## Gifts and Inheritance Specifically

If you inherit money from a parent, or your spouse's family gives you a specific gift, that generally stays your separate property — even though it arrived during the marriage. This surprises some people, since everything else earned during marriage is usually shared. Inheritance and gifts are treated differently on purpose: the law assumes they were meant for one specific person, not the marriage as a whole.

But this protection isn't automatic forever — it depends on what you *do* with that inheritance or gift afterward, which is where the next two sections come in.

## Transmutation: Changing Property's Legal Character on Purpose

**Transmutation** is simply the legal word for deliberately changing what category property belongs to — turning your separate property into shared community property, or the reverse. Under Family Code § 852, this requires:

- A **written** document
- Containing an **express declaration** — meaning it clearly states the intent to change the property's character, not just implies it
- **Signed or otherwise accepted** by the spouse whose interest is being reduced

Here's the part that surprises a lot of people: **a verbal promise doesn't count, no matter how clear it seemed at the time.** If your spouse says "this house is yours now" without a signed document saying so, no transmutation has legally happened — the property keeps its original character. There's one narrow exception: personal gifts between spouses of a personal nature (jewelry, clothing) and not substantial in value relative to the marriage don't need a written agreement.

**Why this rule exists:** without it, an offhand comment or a moment of generosity during an argument could permanently and unintentionally reassign ownership of major property. Requiring a clear, written, signed document protects both spouses from exactly that kind of ambiguity.

## Commingling: When Separate and Shared Money Get Mixed Together

**Commingling** means mixing separate property money with community property money — for example, depositing an inheritance into the same joint checking account you use for household bills — until the two can no longer be clearly told apart.

Here's the important nuance: **commingling alone doesn't automatically turn everything into community property.** California courts apply what's called the **tracing principle** — as long as you can trace the separate funds back to their original source (through bank statements, records showing when the inheritance arrived and how it was used), the separate property character can survive even after being mixed into a shared account.

The problem arises when the mixing becomes so thorough that tracing is genuinely impossible — at that point, courts may treat the whole pool as community property, simply because there's no longer a reliable way to separate out what belonged to whom.

**A related, generally recognized rule: courts often presume community funds were spent first.** In commingled accounts, courts generally presume that everyday family expenses were paid out of community (shared) funds before separate funds were touched. This isn't a specific statute — it's a judicially recognized presumption applied case by case — but it can work in your favor, supporting the argument that your separate inheritance sitting in an account was largely left untouched, rather than assuming it was the first money spent.

## When Community Money Helps Pay for Separate Property — Moore/Marsden

A common real-world scenario: one spouse owned a house before the marriage, but during the marriage, both spouses' income (community property) was used to pay down the mortgage. What happens then?

California uses a specific formula from two cases — *In re Marriage of Moore* (1980) and *In re Marriage of Marsden* (1982) — often just called **Moore/Marsden**, to calculate how much of the home's appreciation the community (the marriage) has earned an interest in.

**A simplified illustration** (real calculations depend on precise mortgage amortization records, so treat this as conceptual, not a formula to apply directly to your own numbers):

- A home is purchased before marriage for $300,000
- During the marriage, community income pays down $40,000 of the mortgage principal
- By the time of divorce, the home has appreciated to $600,000 — a $300,000 gain
- Moore/Marsden calculates the community's *share of that appreciation* based on the proportion of principal the community paid down, relative to the original purchase price

The house itself generally remains the separate property of the spouse who owned it before marriage — but the community can walk away with a real, calculable financial interest in both the principal it paid down and a share of the appreciation, rather than nothing at all. This community interest is a form of the same **reimbursement right** established under Family Code § 2640, which allows a spouse's separate-property or community contributions toward the other's property to be repaid rather than simply absorbed and forgotten.

## Keeping Your Separate Property Protected: Practical Steps

- **Keep inheritance and gifts in a separate account**, not mixed into joint household accounts, if you want to minimize commingling risk
- **Save the paperwork** — the letter or documentation showing a gift or inheritance's source and timing is often the single most useful piece of evidence later
- **Get anything you actually intend as a transmutation in writing** — if you genuinely want to convert separate property into shared property (or vice versa), do it through a clear, signed document, not a conversation
- **Don't assume a title change alone is enough.** Simply adding your spouse's name to a deed can raise real transmutation questions — this is exactly the kind of situation worth a professional consultation before you do it, not after

## Frequently Asked Questions

**If I inherit money and put it in our joint account, do I automatically lose it as separate property?**
Not automatically — if you can trace it back to your inheritance through records, it may still be protected. But keeping it separate from the start avoids the question entirely.

**Can my spouse claim half of an inheritance I received during the marriage?**
Generally no, as long as it stayed clearly identifiable as yours and wasn't transmuted or so thoroughly commingled that tracing became impossible.

**Does adding my spouse's name to the title of my separate property house count as transmutation?**
It can raise exactly that question, and courts take it seriously — this is a genuinely important situation to get professional guidance on before making the change, not after a dispute arises.

**What if we verbally agreed years ago that a property would be "ours" together?**
Under California law, that verbal agreement generally isn't enough on its own — a valid transmutation requires a written, signed document with a clear statement of the change in ownership.
