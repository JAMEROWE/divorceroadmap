If you or your spouse have a 401(k), pension, or IRA, dividing it correctly is one of the highest-stakes pieces of a California divorce — get the paperwork wrong, and it can mean real tax penalties or losing access to funds you're legally entitled to. This is also the piece we flagged as needing its own guide back in the [community property division article](/posts/community-property-division-california/) — here it is.

**Quick Answer: Dividing Retirement Accounts in California**
- Retirement contributions made **during the marriage** are community property under Family Code § 2610, regardless of whose name is on the account
- **401(k)s and pensions require a QDRO** (Qualified Domestic Relations Order) — a separate court order telling the plan administrator how to split the account
- **IRAs do not require a QDRO** — they transfer tax-free between spouses through a different, simpler process
- **Government pensions** (CalPERS, CalSTRS) use a comparable order called a DRO, not technically a QDRO, since those plans fall outside federal ERISA rules
- Pensions are typically divided using the **time rule** — a formula based on how many years of service overlapped with the marriage
- **Don't delay** — an uncompleted QDRO after divorce is one of the most common and costly mistakes people make

## What a QDRO Actually Does

A Qualified Domestic Relations Order is a court order, separate from your divorce judgment, that directs a retirement plan administrator to pay a portion of the benefit to the non-employee spouse — called the "alternate payee" — without triggering the early-withdrawal penalties or immediate tax consequences that would normally apply. Without a QDRO, a plan administrator generally won't release any funds to the other spouse, no matter what your divorce judgment says the split should be.

Each QDRO has to be drafted specifically for the plan it covers — a 401(k) provider and a pension plan have different requirements, and there's no single universal form. This is genuinely one of the more technical pieces of a divorce, and it's an area where getting professional help (a family law attorney or a QDRO specialist) is worth the cost even in an otherwise fully DIY case.

**A required step before any of this: Joinder.** For pensions and many employer plans, California law (Family Code § 2060) requires the retirement plan itself to be formally joined as a party to your divorce case before the court can issue an enforceable order dividing it. Skipping this step means any order the court issues isn't enforceable against the plan — it's a procedural prerequisite, not an optional formality.

## Which Accounts Actually Need One

- **401(k)s, 403(b)s, and most employer-sponsored plans** — require a QDRO under federal ERISA rules (Internal Revenue Code § 414(p))
- **Pensions** — also require a QDRO, though the calculation works differently (see the time rule below)
- **IRAs (traditional, Roth, SEP, SIMPLE)** — do **not** require a QDRO. Under Internal Revenue Code § 408(d)(6), IRA assets can transfer between spouses tax-free as part of a divorce through a direct trustee-to-trustee transfer — but the transfer still needs to be documented correctly, or it can trigger unnecessary taxes
- **CalPERS and CalSTRS (government pensions)** — use a comparable order, generally called a Domestic Relations Order (DRO) rather than a QDRO, since ERISA doesn't apply to government retirement systems. The underlying process is similar, but it goes through the specific plan's own procedures
- **Military retirement** — divided under a separate federal law, the Uniformed Services Former Spouses' Protection Act (USFSPA), not a standard QDRO
- **Federal civilian employee plans (FERS and CSRS)** — also fall outside ERISA, so a standard QDRO will be rejected. These require a **Court Order Acceptable for Processing (COAP)**, submitted to the Office of Personnel Management (OPM), following OPM's specific required language

## The Time Rule for Pensions

Pensions are different from a 401(k) because they promise a future stream of monthly payments rather than a current account balance you can simply split. California courts generally apply the **time rule**, established in *In re Marriage of Brown* (1976) 15 Cal.3d 838:

**Community property fraction = years of service during the marriage ÷ total years of service at retirement**

For example: if someone works 30 total years, and 20 of those years overlap with the marriage, the community property fraction is 20/30 (about 66.7%). Each spouse then generally receives half of that fraction — around 33.3% of the total monthly pension benefit in this example.

One frustrating reality worth knowing upfront: because the time rule applies to a *future* pension payment, the exact dollar value of the community share often can't be pinned down until the employee spouse actually retires. Couples who want a clean, immediate break instead of waiting on this often use the **offset method** — one spouse keeps the retirement account entirely, while the other receives other assets of roughly equivalent value now, avoiding an ongoing QDRO relationship between ex-spouses altogether. This is common in higher-asset divorces where other property can offset the pension's value.

## What Stays Separate Property

Only the portion of a retirement account earned **during the marriage** is community property. Contributions made before the wedding, or after the date of separation, generally remain the separate property of the account holder. Under Family Code § 70, "date of separation" means a complete and final break in the marital relationship — shown both by one spouse expressing intent to end the marriage and by conduct consistent with that intent. This standard (in effect since 2017) replaced an older, stricter rule from *In re Marriage of Davis* (2015) that had required spouses to be living in physically separate residences — meaning couples who'd clearly ended their marriage but hadn't yet moved apart can still establish an earlier separation date today. This is the same date-of-separation principle covered in [community property division](/posts/community-property-division-california/) — it matters here just as much as it does for the rest of your property division.

## Common, Costly Mistakes

- **Delaying the QDRO after the divorce is final.** This is one of the most common and expensive mistakes. If the employee spouse dies before the QDRO is approved ("qualified") by the plan administrator, the plan is generally required to follow whatever beneficiary designation is on file — which can override what your divorce judgment says entirely, leaving the non-employee spouse with no claim on the actual retirement funds even if the paperwork clearly says they're owed a share. Getting the QDRO finished before or immediately after the judgment is final, rather than "getting to it eventually," is the single best protection against this.
- **Using generic language instead of a real QDRO.** A divorce judgment simply stating "retirement accounts will be split equally" isn't enough — the plan administrator needs a specific, plan-compliant order before releasing anything
- **Forgetting IRAs need proper documentation too.** No QDRO is required, but an undocumented or improperly handled IRA transfer can still trigger taxes that a correct trustee-to-trustee transfer would have avoided
- **Not updating beneficiary designations after the divorce** — an old beneficiary designation can override what your divorce agreement says, even years later

## What It Typically Costs

QDRO preparation is one of the few genuinely necessary added costs in an otherwise low-cost DIY divorce — figure roughly $500 to $1,500 per plan, though this varies by preparer and plan complexity, and is worth confirming directly with whoever drafts it for you. This is a real factor in [the overall cost of your divorce](/posts/divorce-cost-california/) if retirement accounts are involved, and it's also relevant if [spousal support](/posts/spousal-support-california/) is part of your case, since retirement assets and income streams can factor into that calculation too.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I split a 401(k) myself without a QDRO?**
No — without a properly drafted and approved QDRO, the plan administrator generally won't release funds to the non-employee spouse, regardless of what your divorce judgment says.

**Do I need a QDRO for an IRA?**
No. IRAs use a different, simpler process — a direct trustee-to-trustee transfer, done correctly under IRS rules to stay tax-free.

**What happens if I don't complete the QDRO right after the divorce?**
You risk losing your claim to the funds entirely if circumstances change (the other spouse's death, a plan change, remarriage) before the order is processed. Complete it as soon as possible, not "eventually."

**Who actually writes the QDRO?**
Typically a family law attorney or a specialized QDRO preparer, since each order has to meet both the specific plan's requirements and federal legal standards. This is a common area to get professional help even in a largely DIY divorce.
