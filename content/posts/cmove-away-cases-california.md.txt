Custody and a parenting plan usually aren't the end of the story. Life changes — a new job, a new relationship, a need to be closer to family — and sometimes one parent wants to move somewhere that would seriously disrupt the existing custody schedule. California has a specific legal framework for exactly this situation, built mostly around two landmark court decisions rather than a single simple rule.

**Quick Answer: How California Handles Move-Away Requests**
- A "move-away" case comes up when a parent wants to relocate in a way that would **significantly disrupt** the current custody arrangement — not just any move
- If one parent has **primary physical custody**, California generally gives them a presumptive right to relocate — established by *In re Marriage of Burgess* (1996), with Family Code § 7501 as the statutory foundation — the other parent must first show the move would actually harm the child
- If custody is **shared roughly equally (joint physical custody)**, courts generally take a **fresh look** at the whole situation using the best-interest standard, without favoring either parent's position
- Once a real risk of harm is shown, courts apply the factors from *In re Marriage of LaMusga* (2004) to decide what to do
- Many custody orders require **written notice at least 45 days before a move** — but check your own order, since this depends on what it actually says, not a fixed universal rule

## What Actually Counts as a "Move-Away" Case

Not every move triggers this process. A move-away case generally means the relocation would meaningfully disrupt the *current* custody and parenting time schedule — moving across town rarely qualifies, while moving to a different state or country almost always does. The practical question courts ask is simple: can the existing parenting plan still function after the move, or does it require an entirely new arrangement?

## Two Very Different Starting Points

This is the single most important thing to understand before anything else: **the legal framework changes dramatically depending on your existing custody arrangement.**

**If one parent has primary physical custody** (the child lives mainly with them under an existing order), California generally recognizes a presumptive right for that parent to relocate. This comes from *In re Marriage of Burgess* (1996) 13 Cal.4th 25, which held that a custodial parent generally has the right to change the child's residence, and the parent objecting to the move carries the burden of showing it would actually harm the child — not just inconvenience them.

**If custody is shared roughly equally (joint physical custody)**, there's no presumption favoring either parent. Instead, courts generally start fresh and decide the whole custody question under the ordinary best-interest standard, treating the proposed move as one factor among many rather than something one parent has a built-in right to do.

Knowing which category your case falls into is the first real step in understanding what you're up against.

## Once Harm Is Shown: The LaMusga Factors

If the objecting parent successfully shows the move could genuinely harm the child, *In re Marriage of LaMusga* (2004) 32 Cal.4th 1072 sets out the framework courts then use to decide what happens next — including a full best-interest analysis, not an automatic denial of the move. The factors courts weigh (this list isn't exhaustive) generally include:

- The child's need for **stability and continuity** in the current arrangement
- The **distance** of the proposed move
- The child's **age**
- The child's relationship with **each parent**
- Each parent's demonstrated ability and willingness to **cooperate and communicate** with the other
- The **reasons for the move** — a legitimate reason (a job transfer, family support, cost of living) is treated very differently from a move that looks like an attempt to interfere with the other parent's contact
- The child's own **wishes**, when the child is old enough for that to be appropriate
- How the move would affect the child's **school, community, and other relationships**

No single factor decides the outcome on its own — courts weigh the whole picture. Importantly, *LaMusga* also confirmed that a court can change custody in response to a proposed move as a way to protect the child's best interest, not as a punishment aimed at the relocating parent.

## The Notice Requirement

Here's a nuance worth getting right, straight from California's own courts guidance: **the 45-day notice rule isn't automatically universal — it depends on what your specific custody order actually says.** Many custody judgments include a provision requiring written notice of a planned move at least 45 days in advance, and California courts commonly include this requirement. But the official guidance is explicit: **check your own judgment or order** to see what it actually requires, rather than assuming a fixed rule applies regardless of your paperwork.

In practice, this means: if your order includes a notice requirement (which is common), follow it exactly. If you're unsure whether yours does, that's worth confirming — with a lawyer or your court's self-help center — before making any moving plans, not after.

Skipping a notice requirement that your order does include, or moving suddenly without following it, is treated seriously — courts can view an unannounced move as evidence the relocating parent was trying to avoid scrutiny, which can work directly against them in the case.

## Why This Connects to the Rest of Your Case

A move-away dispute doesn't exist in isolation — it interacts directly with [your existing custody arrangement](/posts/child-custody-basics-california/) and often requires revisiting [the specific parenting plan](/posts/parenting-plan-california/) you built earlier, since a long-distance schedule looks very different from a local one. A changed custody timeshare can also affect [child support calculations](/posts/child-support-calculation-california/), since the formula factors in how much time each parent actually has with the child. If you're facing this situation, reviewing exactly what your current order actually says about custody type and notice requirements is the necessary first step, since it determines which of the two frameworks above applies to you.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I just move if I have primary custody?**
Not without following the process — you still generally need to give proper notice, and the other parent still has the right to object and ask a court to intervene if they believe the move would harm the child.

**Does joint custody mean I can never move?**
No, but it means there's no presumption in your favor — the court will look at the whole situation fresh, weighing the reasons for the move against the disruption to the existing arrangement.

**What if the other parent just doesn't want me to move, without a real reason the child would be harmed?**
Inconvenience to the other parent alone generally isn't enough — the legal standard focuses on harm to the child, not simply the other parent's preference.

**Can the court really change custody because of a move-away request?**
Yes — this is one of the more significant possible outcomes, and it's treated as a child-protective measure rather than a punishment for wanting to move.
